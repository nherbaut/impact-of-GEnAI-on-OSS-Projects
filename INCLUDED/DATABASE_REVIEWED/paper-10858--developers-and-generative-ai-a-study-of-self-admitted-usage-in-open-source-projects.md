INTRODUCTION: This study investigates how developers self-admit the use of generative AI tools—specifically ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot—within open-source software development. Building on prior work from MSR 2024 focused on ChatGPT, it extends the analysis to Copilot and examines how usage patterns and perceived usefulness have evolved over time.

METHODS: The authors mined GitHub (query date: January 29, 2025) for commits, issues, and pull requests (PRs) mentioning “ChatGPT” or “Copilot,” applied n-gram filtering, removed likely false positives and low-star repositories, and then manually coded statistically significant samples to classify the tasks for which AI assistance was self-admitted. For ChatGPT, 728 candidate artifacts were inspected to obtain 343 validated instances; for Copilot, 933 artifacts were inspected to obtain 281 validated instances (overall from 492 projects). Additional Copilot uses were automatically classified when tool-generated templates or patterns were present (e.g., “generated by Copilot,” “copilot autofix”). Two hierarchical taxonomies were derived through open coding and refinement.

RESULTS: The replicated ChatGPT taxonomy comprises 7 root categories and 61 total categories; Copilot’s comprises 6 root categories and 39 total categories. Frequently admitted uses span feature implementation/enhancement, software quality (debugging, fixing, refactoring, testing), documentation, process support (e.g., PR descriptions), learning, and development environment (e.g., CI integration). For ChatGPT: software quality (100 artifacts) remained the largest category, with increased use for fixing/bug-related support and decreased use for refactoring; learning uses often failed due to hallucinations (incorrect in 73% of such cases). Documentation generation was largely successful (~90%). Feature implementation/enhancement showed ~80% success; development-environment tasks showed ~60% success. For Copilot: extensive, attributable use was observed for process automation (e.g., 1,212 commits and 28,004 PRs with Copilot-generated descriptions; 633 commits and 507 PRs marked “copilot autofix”). Copilot appeared more frequently than ChatGPT in refactoring and testing support. 

DISCUSSION: Self-admitted traces provide a partial and biased view focused on instances developers chose to disclose or where tools auto-annotated outputs. Results suggest complementary roles: IDE-integrated Copilot is favored for context-rich, repetitive tasks tightly coupled to repositories and CI, while ChatGPT is often used for broader reasoning tasks (e.g., debugging discussions, documentation, brainstorming) but is prone to hallucinations for technology-learning queries. The study highlights implications for practice (e.g., adapting review processes, prompt engineering needs, ownership concerns) and for research (e.g., difficulty distinguishing AI- vs human-written code, need for new methods to capture AI involvement).
